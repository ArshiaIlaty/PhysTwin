Inverse Force Control
on Deformable Objects

Given a deformable object's current state and a goal force — on a rope, a sheet of cloth, a plush toy — we learn the gripper motion that produces it. We introduce two methods: an open-loop policy that acts from state and goal alone, and a closed-loop policy that also senses the force it is currently applying.

Arshia Ilaty · Malak Gamal El-Din

University of California, Irvine · CS 175 / CS 275

GitHub PhysTwin Paper

Overview

Our goal is contact-force control on deformable objects: move the gripper, frame by frame, so the force on the object matches a target trajectory F*(t) — across objects whose stiffness varies by orders of magnitude. We solve it with two methods, open-loop and closed-loop control.

Everything runs inside PhysTwin, which reconstructs each object from RGB-D video as a calibrated spring-mass digital twin. The twin's spring forces are our ground-truth force labels — exact, dense, and available every frame with no physical sensor or hardware anywhere in the pipeline.

Novel contributions

The contribution is the setting: commanded-force tracking on a per-video-calibrated deformable twin, spanning a wide stiffness range. No neighboring line of work takes a force trajectory as the task specification:

Problem Setup

Current state + goal force in → gripper motion out. Each frame, the policy reads the object's current state and a goal force, and outputs a 6D gripper displacement so the contact force on the object matches the goal. We do this with two methods — open-loop and closed-loop control.

Goal Track a target force trajectory F*(t) in a live rollout Output 6D gripper displacement — how to move each hand this frame Ground-truth force labels Spring forces from the calibrated PhysTwin simulator — no physical force sensors Metric err_ratio: tracking error relative to the goal's size — 0 = perfect · 1 = error as large as the goal · >1 = uncontrolled

Two methods — one difference in input

Open-loop no feedback

  • Object particle statistics that describe its current deformation
  • Goal force per gripper
  • Material descriptor

Acts from state and goal alone — never sees the force it actually produces.

Closed-loop force feedback

  • Everything the open-loop policy sees, plus:
  • ＋ Current achieved force per gripper

Feedback lets it sense its own tracking error and correct it frame by frame.

Particle statistics summarize hundreds of particle positions into a fixed-size vector: shape change, motion, and gripper-contact distances.

Evaluation — replay and ramp

Both methods are evaluated in live rollouts under two kinds of goal:

Replay in-distribution

F*(t) is a recorded force trajectory from the dataset.

Tests: can the policy reproduce forces it has seen examples of?

Ramp out-of-distribution

F*(t) is a synthetic ramp — 0 → peak → 0 — never seen in training.

Tests: can the policy track a completely novel goal?

The ramp is hard for one specific reason: its descending half requires the gripper to retract — and human demonstrations never retract on purpose.

Three levels of generalization

  1. Frame-level. Hold out one contiguous time window per case (a "random block") for validation. Contiguity matters — interleaved frames would leak answers through velocity features.
  2. Trajectory-level. Roll out on a goal invented at test time (the ramp). New goal, new motion, new particle evolution.
  3. Material-level. Hold an entire material out of training and rely on the material descriptor to transfer (Experiment 10).

A recurring lesson: validation loss does not predict rollout performance. Several "improvements" lowered validation loss and then destroyed control. Every claim on this page is backed by live rollouts.

The data

  • Force scales span ~40× across the dataset — one network must operate in what amounts to three different unit systems.
  • Stiffness alone does not separate the materials — rope, cloth, and plush overlap in stiffness but separate by force scale. This drives both the material descriptor below and the leakage problem we later uncover in it (Experiment 6).

The material descriptor

One policy network handles all three materials, so it needs a hint about what it is holding. We give it a two-number fingerprint — how stiff the object is and what force scale the episode operates at (both on log scales, since they vary by orders of magnitude). A stiff object generates more force for the same motion, so the policy should move less per unit of desired force change.

Closed-Loop Force Control

The closed-loop policy is the one that reads the force it is currently producing. Every frame: read the state, the achieved force, and the goal; output a displacement; the simulator advances and the new force feeds back into the next decision.

Why feedback matters — teacher forcing vs student forcing

Training data shows the policy only correct states (teacher forcing). At deployment the policy drives the simulation itself (student forcing): one imperfect move puts it in a state it never trained on, and errors compound. This distinction explains almost every failure mode below — and force feedback is the policy's only way to notice its own drift.

Running example — double_lift_cloth_1

  1. t = 0 Cloth hangs between two grippers at rest, contact force ≈ 0. The policy reads the particle statistics, zero achieved force, the first goal force, and the cloth material descriptor.
  2. t = 1…T Each frame it outputs a 6D nudge; springs stretch; a new force comes back. It compares achieved to goal and adjusts. The gripper trajectory is entirely the policy's own — not from any demonstration.
  3. eval err_ratio over the full rollout = 0.22 for this case (BC best) — the average tracking error is 22% of the goal's magnitude.

The ramp failure is not a fluke. The BC policy was trained only on human demonstrations, which never require deliberate retraction. When the goal drops back to zero, the policy is in an unseen state and has no learned behavior for pulling back. Release fraction (fraction of frames where force correctly decreases as goal decreases): BC = 0.04. It barely moves.

The hard case — a ramp goal, phase by phase

Same loop, but the goal is an invented ramp: pull with a force that rises to a peak and returns to zero. Read each row as "what the policy sees → what it must do":

PhaseFramesWhat the policy readsCorrect behaviorWhat you see in the video
Grasp0–5 F(t) ≈ 0, F* small tiny tightening moves achieved-force arrow starts to grow
Rising edge5–45 F(t) < F*(t+1), gap growing pull harder each frame colored arrow chases the dashed goal arrow up the ramp
Peak~46 F(t) ≈ F* ≈ 17 kN hold arrows co-located
Falling edge — the hard part46–92 F(t) = high, F*(t+1) = low retract to shed force BC stalls here (release fraction 0.04 — keeps pulling); RL reverses the gripper (0.87)

The falling edge is the whole point: "force is high now, goal is low next" is the one situation that never appears in demonstration data, so an imitation policy has no behavior for it.

Open Loop vs Closed Loop — When Does Feedback Matter?

Our two methods differ by a single input: the achieved force. To compare them fairly, both are trained on the same dataset, split, architecture, and epochs — only the feedback input changes. How much is feedback actually worth? The answer splits cleanly by evaluation mode:

  • Replay: removing feedback barely changes performance. When the goal looks like training data, the goal alone determines the right action.
  • Ramp: removing feedback costs +1.48 err_ratio on cloth and +0.65 on sloth. Under a novel goal the state drifts away from anything in training, and only the closed-loop policy can sense that drift and correct it. The open-loop policy — blind to the force it is holding — finishes at 2–3× the residual force.

Feedback's value is concentrated at the edge of the training distribution — exactly where RL's value also shows up. Both attack the same failure from different sides.

Experiments & Improvements

The policy work proceeded in stages — each experiment fixes a failure mode found in the previous one. The pipeline below is shared by all; what changes is the architecture, the conditioning, and the training signal.

1 — Behavior Cloning Baseline

cloth replay 0.36 · rope replay 0.75 · cloth ramp 6.55 · sloth ramp 5.06

The idea: imitate the gripper motion in recorded demonstrations and synthetic rollouts. Replay performance is decent — the policy reproduces forces it has seen examples of. Ramp performance is catastrophic: the policy never saw states where it needed to retract, so it simply doesn't.

2 — Reinforcement Learning (PPO warm-started from BC)

rope replay 0.41 · rope ramp 0.52 · release fraction 0.87

The idea: instead of imitating, reward the policy each frame for how closely its achieved force matches the goal, and let it learn from its own rollouts. Because RL trains on the states the policy itself produces, the release problem disappears — release fraction jumps from 0.04 to 0.87. The gripper now retracts when the goal drops.

3 — 2D Material Descriptor (1D stiffness → 2D stiffness + force scale)

rope replay: 0.75 → 0.46  |  sloth replay: 0.87 → 0.68

The BC baseline used a single material category one-hot. This doesn't tell the policy how stiff the object is, or what force magnitude to expect. We replaced it with [log(spring_stiffness), log(mean_force)]. The log scale was chosen because stiffness varies by orders of magnitude across materials; a raw difference isn't useful. Replay performance improved substantially — particularly for rope and sloth, where the force scale mismatch with cloth was largest.

4 — Transformer Backbone + Scheduled Sampling

cloth replay 0.305 · rope replay 0.391 — both better than RL

The idea: give the policy memory — a transformer that sees several past frames and a short preview of the commanded goal. Alone, this won replay but catastrophically regressed on ramps (sloth ramp: 29.5): a more expressive imitation model amplifies its own compounding errors.

The fix: inject noise into the previous-action input during training, so the model learns to recover from its own small errors. Sloth ramp collapses 29.5 → 2.25, and replay beats the RL policy. The clearest lesson of the project: the right fix for compounding errors is to train under them — not to add RL.

5 — FiLM Conditioning + Enriched Physics Descriptor

sloth ramp 1.38 — all-time project best  |  beats/ties RL on 5 of 6 groups

The idea: instead of feeding the material descriptor in as just another input, FiLM uses it to modulate the network's activations at every layer — pushing each material into its own region of the network rather than letting it be memorized. An enriched physics descriptor (separate stiffnesses, friction, elasticity) improves every OOD ramp: sloth ramp reaches 1.38, the project's all-time best. The alternative — a per-case embedding token — was harmful: the model memorizes case identity instead of generalizing.

6 — Leakage Audit: the Material Descriptor Was Partly Cheating

honest negative result — rope replay collapses 0.46 → 0.84 without force scale

The descriptor's force-scale component comes from the recorded episode — which, on replay tests, is essentially the target trajectory itself. The policy was getting a hint about the answer. Removing the force scale collapsed rope replay 0.46 → 0.84; adding it back recovered the results exactly.

What we thought we learned: material conditioning helps the policy understand material physics and calibrate its strategy.
What actually happened: the policy was reading the goal's force magnitude from the descriptor and using it as a shortcut. Stiffness alone — the honest material signal — is real but weaker, because force magnitude is set mostly by the commanded goal, not by material stiffness.

This is an important negative result: it tells us that the architecture is not wrong (the encoder can reproduce the descriptor exactly), but that naive multi-material conditioning can introduce subtle information leakage that inflates in-distribution metrics.

7 — RL on Top of the Transformer Adds Nothing (measured negative result)

PPO fine-tuning of the noise-injected transformer: identical to warm-start on all 6 groups

RL fine-tuning of the noise-injected transformer (same recipe that worked earlier) produced checkpoints identical to the warm-start on all 6 evaluation groups. The noise injection already gives imitation training the coverage RL used to provide — there is nothing left for RL to fix. RL's lasting contribution was diagnosing that distribution shift was the problem; the cleaner fix was simulating that shift in supervised training.

Pushing Further — Ensemble, Diagnosis & Generalization

The experiments above produced two complementary specialists — the transformer (best on replay) and the FiLM physics policy (best on ramps). Three follow-up questions: can we combine them? · where does the remaining error live? · does the policy generalize to an unseen material?

8 — Goal-Aware Policy Ensemble (no training, no extra simulator calls)

14-case mean err_ratio 0.66 — beats every single policy (best single 0.71), within 0.03 of the per-case oracle

The idea: we always know our own commanded goal, so route each rollout to the right specialist before running it. One cheap question — does the goal return to zero after its peak? — cleanly separates ramp-shaped goals (→ FiLM policy) from replay-shaped goals (→ transformer), 14/14 cases routed correctly — with one material-aware exception (cloth ramps route to the RL policy, where FiLM regresses). No look-ahead, no oracle labels, no new training. The ensemble matches or beats the best single policy on all six groups, within 0.03 of an oracle allowed to peek at every policy's result.

9 — Where the Error Lives: Project Progression and Per-Axis Diagnosis

Fy (lift axis) is the hardest component everywhere · cloth ramp is the standing frontier

Reading the whole project as one chart: each idea moved specific groups. Material conditioning collapsed the ramp disasters (cloth ramp 6.6 → 2.3, sloth ramp 5.1 → 1.4); the transformer plus scheduled sampling won replay; FiLM physics won the rope/sloth ramps. The deployable ensemble (green dashed) is the per-group floor of this whole sequence. Cloth ramp is the one group that never breaks below ~2.3.

Breaking the residual error down by force axis reveals a consistent physical story: the vertical lift axis (Fy) is the hardest to control for every material, and dramatically so for the heavy plush sloth (normalized error 1.36 on Fy vs 0.45 on Fz). The in-plane push axes (Fx, Fz) are far easier. This is intuitive — the lift axis fights gravity and drives the large deformation/release dynamics, exactly the regime where compounding error bites.

Limitations & Future Work

Limitations — stated plainly

  • Everything lives inside the twin. Force ground truth is the calibrated simulator's spring force, so all results inherit PhysTwin's per-case calibration accuracy. There is no sim-to-real transfer claim here; the force magnitudes (kN-scale) are simulator units, not realistic hand forces.
  • Cloth ramp is unsolved. Every policy — and the per-case oracle over all of them — plateaus at err_ratio ≈ 2.3. We can reduce force on the descending ramp but consistently overshoot it.
  • Small case count. ~17 distinct cases means per-case conditioning richer than ~5–6 dims starts memorizing case identity instead of material physics (measured in the descriptor-dimension sweep).
  • Held-out-material transfer is partial. A never-seen material is controllable on gentle goals (0.47) but degrades 2–4× on aggressive ones (Experiment 10).
  • A noise floor from the demonstrations themselves. The recorded human gripper motion is not rigid (~1.4 mm/frame intra-cluster deviation), which caps how precisely any imitation-derived policy can act.

Future work

  • Crack the cloth-ramp frontier — every policy and the per-case oracle plateau at err_ratio ≈ 2.3 on cloth ramp (Experiment 8). Adding cloth-ramp data helps cloth but regresses rope (a measured shared-capacity trade-off). The next move is per-material output heads or per-material data weighting so cloth-ramp coverage doesn't crowd out rope.
  • MPC upper bound — the goal-aware ensemble gives an empirical per-case oracle, but it is still bounded by the policies we trained. Model-predictive control with full differentiable- simulator access would give a true control ceiling — especially diagnostic on cloth ramp, to tell whether the plateau is a policy limit or a physics limit.
  • Target the Fy (lift) axis — the per-axis diagnosis (Experiment 9) shows the vertical lift axis dominates the error budget on every material. An axis-weighted loss or reward, or an explicit gravity-compensation term, could attack the error where it actually concentrates.
  • Scaling the BC dataset — 14 real cases and 218 synthetic trajectories is small. The synthetic generation pipeline is already in place; more diverse motion families would improve coverage of the ramp distribution and could strengthen held-out-material transfer.
  • Context adapter (VLA-style) — inject an external task description (e.g., "lift gently") into the policy as a learned conditioning vector. We tested adapter tokens and found them harmful; a summary-injection approach (external to the attention sequence) may avoid the memorization problem.
